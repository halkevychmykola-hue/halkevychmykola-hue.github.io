The thread through all three

Write the hypothesis first, then try to break it

  1. In 2025 I built my first automated system on Interactive Brokers. Its backtests looked strong, and they turned out to be overstated.

  2. That taught me the rule behind everything since: write the expected result before the test, freeze the thresholds, and test on data the model has never seen.

  3. I applied it to three areas: intraday structure of Nasdaq-100 futures, distress risk in UK listed companies, and commodity futures after physical supply shocks.

  4. Ideas that fail these tests are rejected and documented; the ones that held are below, with their limits.

A multi-scale market model for Nasdaq-100 futures

Open the interactive model: market simulator, day replay, axis space, 60 strategies →

At any moment, from one minute to one month ahead, what is the most effective action after costs, including doing nothing? The model does not forecast prices.

What I built

  • Data pipeline. About 1.8 million one-minute bars of Nasdaq-100 futures (NQ), regular session, September 2007 to December 2025: 4,704 trading days. Added Treasury futures (ZN), crude oil (CL), the VIX, the VXN volatility index, CFTC positioning data and a tick-level sample with buyer- and seller-initiated trades.
  • State space. 37 minute-level dimensions: momentum, market "clock speed" and volume tempo, the Hurst exponent, entropy, novelty and cross-market links. On top, 29 slower layers: tail risk, liquidity and the obligations of large participants, such as leveraged-ETF rebalancing, CTA and volatility-target funds, pension rebalancing and the volatility surface.
  • Simulated market. A browser-based simulator with modelled participants: pension rebalancing, leveraged-ETF flows, forced sellers and an options market. It runs 15 years in about 1.7 seconds. Every strategy is checked there first: switch off its mechanism, and its edge should disappear.
  • Strategy library. 59 futures and options strategies across all horizons, a portfolio builder, and transfers of the framework to Apple stock and crude oil.

How I validated it

  • Expected results written down before every test.
  • Thresholds fixed on 2011–2016, then tested once on 2017–2025.
  • Placebo controls: the same features shifted three years back.
  • Standard errors clustered by day, and realistic broker costs of 0.5 index points per round trip.
  • S&P 500 futures (ES) kept untouched as a final hold-out.

Key findings

FindingEvidence
Costs, not direction, decide short horizonsCost is about 14% of a typical 1-minute move but about 1.3% of a 2-hour move (2017–25).
Minute-level direction is close to noise; size and volume are predictableHurst exponent of direction 0.49–0.51, against 0.72–0.94 for absolute moves. The model ranks the size of the next move well (rank correlation 0.3–0.5) but not its sign.
Order flow explains the present, not the futureNet aggressive buying explains about half of the same minute's move (correlation 0.52–0.56) and about nothing of the next minutes.
One robust effect: strong days continue, driven by forced flows"Follow the day for 4 hours" was positive in each of 9 out-of-sample years, strongest when leveraged ETFs must rebalance and the VIX is falling. The same rule passed on Apple 2020–25: +5.2 bp per trade after costs, t = 2.0.
Selling options pays only at a good priceShort-option strategies pay off only while the option price stays above about 0.60–0.67× the VXN level, intraday and at one day.
In my words · most unexpected

Time is not the same for different market participants. For a high-frequency trader, a pension fund and a leveraged ETF that rebalances at the close, one hour means completely different things. The second surprise: a normal price chart, price against time, is a two-dimensional picture. It reflects neither real time nor the real market, because the market is a multi-dimensional object.

In my words · open problem

I managed to describe the market in many dimensions and to model time differently for different participants. But I still haven't answered one question: how to build a clear strategy without falling back into the standard financial paradigm and collapsing the whole project into the usual two-dimensional view of price against time.

Rejected models and limits

A model fed every feature at once showed a Sharpe ratio of 2.0–2.8 in-sample and fell to about zero or below out of sample. The protocol caught it, and I rejected and documented it. No single strategy is statistically proven yet: the strategy-level Sharpe is about 0.5 ± 0.7. The framework, the validation discipline and the negative results are the achievement.

Tail: a distress and credit-risk model for UK companies

Open the interactive model: Blotto game and 48 companies →

For 48 UK listed companies, how likely is a forced painful step (a rights issue, a dividend cut or a restructuring) and a 50–70% share-price fall before an option expires? And does the options market price that risk correctly?

What I built

  • Coverage. 48 companies: 10 banks, 8 property companies (REITs), 8 housebuilders, 7 utilities, 6 insurers, 4 asset managers, 3 government contractors and 2 consumer names. 44 analysed from reported metrics, 4 built by hand from primary documents, such as Standard Life and Unite Students.
  • UK credit web. A map of how stress spreads through the UK system: gilts, private credit, funded reinsurance, commercial property and bank capital. Each scenario comes with a measurable sensor, for example the 10-year gilt yield above 5.5% for two weeks.
  • Fault tree per company. AND/OR logic from the shock to the forced step, built from balance-sheet tests: refinancing wall, cash runway, interest cover, CET1 headroom over the MDA threshold, Solvency II ratio and LTV covenant headroom. Every node carries a probability, its source (data, model or estimate) and a sensor to watch.
  • Timing model. A stochastic path model with a Bayesian update of current pressure, which gives when the step is likely and how the price travels there.
  • Market comparison and sizing. Model probability against option-implied probability (from option prices for 11 names, from volatility for the rest), then quarter-Kelly sizing for buying puts, with a spread penalty and caps of 5% per name and 10% in total.
  • Built-in self-checks. Warnings when a sector risk is used as a company risk, when a proxy replaces the real target, when long AND-chains lose probability, and when the company's own defences are ignored.

Results

  • For most companies the model and the option market roughly agree: St James's Place 12.4% against 12.2%, Babcock 11.2% against 11.6%.
  • The model flags a few names as riskier than options imply: Standard Life 16.6% against 4.4%, Vanquis 8.4% against 1.4%, Capita 34.8% against 26.5%.
  • Only 3 positions get a positive Kelly size, 2.8% of capital in total. Under the most pessimistic placeholder parameters almost nothing survives, and the model reports that openly.

Beyond the UK: 358 lines of defence

To see where the UK sits, I mapped the defences of 20 countries and the EU level: UK, France, Sweden, Germany, the Netherlands, Belgium, Ireland, Switzerland, Austria, Italy, Spain, Portugal, Norway, Denmark, Finland, Japan, South Korea, Mexico, Brazil and South Africa. That gave 358 "walls" (central-bank backstops, capital buffers, mortgage rules, deposit guarantees, fiscal rules), each with the assumption it relies on and what it does not cover, plus 41 external shocks and 200 links between walls inside each country. I model it as a Colonel Blotto game: a shock breaks a wall when its force exceeds the wall's strength.

In my words · most unexpected

At the company level there were few real surprises. What surprised me was the system. Banks and governments keep preparing for the last war. In a Colonel Blotto game, they put their resources where the last crisis hit, so the break happens in places that were considered safe, because no one was defending them. And this is not only a UK problem. The same pattern shows up almost everywhere: in the UK the weak spot is gilts and long-term bonds, in Sweden it is property, in France it is credit, the banking system and mortgages.

In my words · my mistake

The first problem I hit was substitution of concepts: quietly replacing one thing with another, like treating a sector risk as a company risk, or measuring a proxy instead of the real target. I fixed it with mathematical logic. Each company got a fault tree with explicit AND/OR links, and every node had to name its source: data, model or estimate.

Limits
  • Several parameters are placeholders awaiting calibration. History is used only as a check, not for fitting.
  • In the country map, wall strength and shock force are still ordinal estimates, not money amounts, and there are no links between countries yet. Only the UK has a company-level model.
  • Position sizes are hypothetical. No positions were taken.

Circuit-1: event-driven macro and commodity futures

Open the interactive 2022 replay: every trade, rules vs me →

Can a disciplined process trade commodity and currency futures only after a real physical supply shock (a war hitting a supplier, sanctions with a start date, an export ban, a blocked route) and stay out the rest of the time? Typically that means 2–3 ideas a year.

What I built

  • Trigger map. 9 event classes: war, dated sanctions, export bans, blockades, politically forced shutdowns, trade wars on a key flow, cartel break-ups, country-specific economic shocks, and accidents or extreme weather. Each lists the markets it hits hardest and what does not count.
  • Severity test. 5 questions before any trade: what physically breaks and how much; is there a dated mechanism; who replaces the supply and how fast; what will show the disruption has faded; which market expresses it most cleanly. Proposed threshold: at least 3% of world supply, 10% of a region's imports or 20% of world exports.
  • Event lifecycle. Threat → dated mechanism → disruption → adaptation → normalisation. Entry is allowed only at stages 2–3; adaptation means exit.
  • Risk rules. One event is one idea and one unit of risk (1R). 1R = daily volatility (63 sessions) × √20. Stop at −1R, monthly loss limit −1R, at most two events at once.
  • Date-locked replay of 2022. A cursor moves through 2022 week by week. Only facts dated on or before the cursor are shown, and I make every decision. All decisions go into a journal (472 rows).
  • Hypothesis machine c1m (C++20). Each monthly protocol is compiled and sealed with a SHA-256 hash before the data arrives. Data collectors for Interactive Brokers, EIA, FRED, CFTC COT, USDA WASDE and NOAA weather. It handles the mechanics of holding and exits.
  • Hypothesis standard. Built on Popper and pre-registration: arguments for and against plus a null hypothesis, numeric kill conditions, and my own probabilities scored with the Brier score.

The edge rests on a few very large events and is not statistically proven (t ≈ 1.2). The event list was assembled in hindsight, so the next step is a blind radar in replay and forward paper trading.

In my words · most unexpected

How big the gap is between building a hypothesis and actually executing it. You have to separate the idea from holding and managing the position, because stress affects you a lot while you are in a trade, and it eats a large part of the profit. In the replay my own decisions made +1.45R, while the same trades with rule-based exits made +6.5R. That showed me where the money was lost: in execution, not in the ideas.

In my words · my mistake

I underestimated execution stress. Managing these futures positions is genuinely hard, and stress was the main problem. The lesson: the idea and the execution have to be separated, and execution needs rules, not feelings. That is why the machine now handles the mechanics, and I keep the thesis and the decision.

Tests I pre-registered and rejected
  • Driver variables give no out-of-sample direction (information coefficient ≈ 0).
  • News enters the price on the day it comes out. After the first day, a shock is a coin flip.
  • A mechanical entry after the USDA WASDE report: rejected. Weekly EIA data adds almost nothing (about +0.03R).
  • Option overlays, a bubble detector (BSADF), straddles after a crash, holding longer, forced-seller trades: none passed the pre-registered rule.
  • "Fear" trades in the yen, Swiss franc and short AUD: 52 trades, −24.3R, dropped.
Limits
  • The 2010–22 event list was built in hindsight, and the result depends on two events.
  • The 2022 replay rules were refined on the same trades they were tested on.
  • All checks were done by me; there was no external review. Next step: blind replay and forward paper trading.
Across all three projects

What I learned

In my words · what I will take into a bank or fund

The most useful thing I learned is mathematical logic as a way to evaluate myself and my own actions. I stopped substituting one concept for another. I now think more structurally, not only about my actions but about my thoughts and concepts, and I break them down clearly with mathematical logic.

In my words · main mistake

Overfitting. I kept running into the same problem: backtests fitted to the past. My first system in 2025 looked strong on paper, but the results were overstated. In the NQ project, a model fed every feature at once showed a Sharpe ratio of 2.8 in-sample and fell to about zero out of sample. That is why every project since has rules written before the test, frozen thresholds and checks on data the model has never seen.

In my words · why I paused the projects

Each of these projects has a good seed, and any of them could grow into a start-up of its own, especially the NQ model and the Tail stress system. The main problem is that I can't combine the engineer, the strategist, the risk manager and the financier in one person. Put simply, I can't be a fund on my own. I can build the system, but then it has to be run, and running it takes completely different skills. On top of that, these projects need far more capital than I have. So I have frozen them: everything is documented and working. For now I'm focusing on internships and building my own experience, and I can come back to them later.
